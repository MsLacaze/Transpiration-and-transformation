MISSION 1

💧 The great water trail

Plants need water for photosynthesis, transporting dissolved minerals and keeping their cells firm. Roots absorb water from the soil. It travels upwards through xylem, a transport tissue that forms a continuous pathway through roots, stems and leaves.

Inside leaves, water evaporates and becomes water vapour. The vapour diffuses out through tiny pores called stomata. This loss of water vapour is transpiration. Evaporation creates tension (a pulling force) in the xylem. Cohesion makes water molecules stick to each other, while adhesion makes them stick to xylem walls. Together these help keep water moving upwards.

🚪 Stomata lab: open or shut?

Stomata are tiny pores, usually on leaves. Carbon dioxide enters through them for photosynthesis; oxygen and water vapour can also pass out. Each pore is controlled by two guard cells. When guard cells take in water they become firm (turgid) and the stoma opens. When they lose water they become less firm (flaccid) and the stoma closes. Open stomata help gas exchange but allow water vapour to escape. Closed stomata conserve water but limit carbon dioxide entry.

🌬️ Weather control room

Four environmental factors change transpiration. Temperature: warmer conditions usually speed evaporation. Humidity: humid air slows diffusion of water vapour out of leaves. Wind: removes moist air around leaves, increasing water loss. Light: usually encourages stomata to open for photosynthesis, allowing more water vapour to escape.

MISSION 4

🌳 Forest detective

The canopy is the upper layer of a forest formed by tree crowns. Trees release water vapour through transpiration. Evaporation uses energy and can cool leaves and nearby air. Water vapour from vegetation can contribute to cloud formation and rainfall, depending on wider weather conditions. The canopy creates a microclimate – local environmental conditions. Shade can keep the forest floor cooler and often more humid. Removing forest can change local temperatures, shade and water movement.

MISSION 5

🧪 Experiment zone

A leafy plant cutting can be placed inside a clear plastic bag or transparent container, sealed around the stem and left in a suitable light location. Water vapour released by leaves can collect as droplets on the inside surface. A control setup without leaves helps check whether droplets come from the plant rather than another source. Droplets show water vapour has been released and condensed, but do not measure the exact rate of transpiration.

🔢 Stomatal density dash

Scientists can estimate stomatal density by making a leaf impression, viewing it under a microscope and counting stomata in a known area. Density means the number of stomata per square millimetre. Divide the number counted by the area examined.

28 stomata ÷ 0.2 mm² = 140 stomata/mm²

🌵 Adaptation match-up

Hydrophytes are water plants. Floating leaves often have stomata on top; submerged leaves may have none. Mesophytes live in moderately moist habitats and often have most stomata on the lower leaf surface. Xerophytes live in dry habitats and may have sunken stomata, thick cuticles or rolled leaves to reduce water loss.
